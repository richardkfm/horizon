# Look after your mind and your grief over the weeks after

After a loss, a shock, or a long stretch of stress, the first days are about
getting through — see [[health-psychological-first-aid]]. This guide is for
the weeks and months that follow, when the urgency fades but the weight
doesn't. Feeling low, tired, irritable, or numb after something hard is a
normal human response, not a weakness. Most people slowly find their feet
again, and the same small habits that help in hard times keep us well in
ordinary ones.

> **Do now:** If someone talks about wanting to die or to end their life,
> take it seriously and act today — see **If someone is thinking of
> suicide** below.

## Grief has no timetable

- **It comes in waves.** Good days and bad days mix, often for a long time.
  A song, a smell, or an anniversary can bring it back sharply. That's
  normal.
- **There's no right way.** Some people cry a lot, some don't cry at all,
  some get busy, some go quiet.
- **Grief isn't only for deaths.** People also grieve a home, a job, health,
  a community, or a way of life.
- **Remembering helps.** Talking about the person or what was lost, sharing
  stories, marking anniversaries together — see [[culture-storytelling]].

## Five daily anchors

When everything feels shaky, small steady habits give the mind something to
hold on to. Don't aim for all five perfectly — pick one and start.

```ascii
               +------------+
               |   SLEEP    |
               +-----+------+
                     |
   +-----------+   +-+--------+   +-----------+
   |  ROUTINE  |---|   YOU    |---|  MOVING   |
   +-----------+   +-+--------+   +-----------+
                     |
               +-----+------+
               | CONNECTION |
               +-----+------+
                     |
               +-----+------+
               |  PURPOSE   |
               +------------+
```

*Fig. 1: five daily anchors — each one steadies the others; start with
whichever feels easiest today*

1. **Sleep:** go to bed and get up at roughly the same times. Get daylight in
   the morning, keep caffeine to the morning, and go easy on alcohol — it
   helps you fall asleep but wrecks the second half of the night.
2. **Routine:** regular meals, a fixed time to get dressed and start the day.
   A simple plan for tomorrow, written down, makes it less daunting.
3. **Movement:** a daily walk, gardening, cycling, dancing — anything that
   raises your heart rate a little. Outdoors and with others is even better.
4. **Connection:** talk to someone every day, even briefly. Accept offers of
   help, and offer some. Loneliness makes everything heavier.
5. **Purpose:** a small useful task — cooking for someone, mending, growing
   food, helping a neighbour — gives a sense of control back.

> **Tip:** Limit the news. Check it once or twice a day at set times, from a
> trusted source, not in bed. Constant scrolling feeds anxiety without making
> anyone safer.

## Start a support circle

People in the same street or group often share the same losses. A regular
circle lets them carry it together.

- **Meet regularly,** at the same time and place — weekly works well.
- **Keep it small,** about 5–10 people, and sit in a circle.
- **Agree simple rules:** what's said here stays here; one person speaks at a
  time; listen without fixing or advising unless asked; anyone can pass.
- **Go round the circle** so everyone gets a turn, quiet people included.
- **Rotate who hosts,** so it doesn't depend on one person.
- **Know where to point people** who need more than a circle can give.

## Children's reactions

Children grieve and worry too, but they often show it through behaviour
rather than words.

- **Common reactions:** clinginess, nightmares, bedwetting or "going back" to
  younger behaviour, tummy aches, anger, or replaying the event in play.
  These usually settle with time and steady care.
- **Tell the truth, simply,** in words they can understand. Use real words
  like "died" rather than "went to sleep", which can frighten them.
- **Keep routines going** — meals, bedtime, school or lessons, play.
- **Let them ask questions,** more than once, and let them see that adults
  feel sad too and are coping.
- **Play and make things together** — see [[culture-children-play-learning]].

## When to seek more help

> **Risk:** Talk to a doctor, counsellor, or health worker if, after a few
> weeks, someone:
> - can't manage daily basics like eating, washing, or caring for others
> - feels hopeless, worthless, or numb most of the day
> - has intense flashbacks, nightmares, or panic that aren't easing
> - is drinking or using drugs more to cope
> - is getting worse rather than slowly better
>
> Asking for help is a strong, sensible step, not a failure.

## If someone is thinking of suicide

You don't need training to help. What helps most is to notice, ask, and
stay.

1. **Ask directly.** "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Asking does
   not put the idea in their head — it shows you can hear the answer.
2. **Listen** without judging, arguing, or rushing to fix things.
3. **Stay with them,** or make sure someone they trust is with them. Don't
   leave them alone if they're in danger now.
4. **Make the place safer:** with their agreement, remove or lock away
   anything they could use to harm themselves, such as stored medicines,
   ropes, or blades.
5. **Get help now:** call emergency services if they are in immediate
   danger. Otherwise, contact a crisis line together, or a doctor.
6. **Follow up** the next day and the days after. Staying in touch matters.

> **Note:** Look up your local crisis line now and write it on your
> household list and in the [[checklist:home-emergency-prep]] checklist —
> for example, 988 in the US, or Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland.
> Numbers differ by country; find yours before you need it.

## Look after the carers

Supporting someone through grief or depression is draining. Share the
load across several people, take breaks without guilt, and use the same
anchors — sleep, movement, connection — for yourself.

# Save, reuse, and share water day to day

Most homes use far more water than they need, without noticing. Saving it
isn't about going without — it's about stopping leaks, using each litre more
than once, and knowing exactly what you'd cut first if supply ever got tight.
A household that already lives this way saves money in ordinary years and
barely notices a hosepipe ban or a dry summer.

> **Decision:** If water ever runs short, protect it in this order —
> **drinking**, then **cooking**, then **handwashing and basic hygiene**,
> then **washing clothes and dishes**, then **gardens and everything else**.
> Never cut drinking water to stretch a supply; dehydration is dangerous,
> especially for babies, older people, and anyone ill or working in heat.

## Step 1: Audit where your water goes

You can't save what you can't see. Spend one ordinary day measuring.

1. **Read the meter** last thing at night and first thing in the morning. If
   it moved while nobody used water, you have a leak.
2. **Check the toilet:** put a few drops of food colouring in the cistern and
   don't flush for 15 minutes. Colour in the bowl means a leaking valve —
   often the single biggest silent waste in a home.
3. **Time the big uses** with a bucket and a watch: fill a 10-litre bucket
   from the shower or tap and note how many seconds it takes. Then you know
   what a 5-minute shower really costs.
4. **Write a rough daily list** — showers, flushes, laundry loads, cooking,
   garden — so you can see the big items. [[calc-water-budget]] walks through
   the numbers.

> **Tip:** A dripping tap can waste thousands of litres a year. Replacing a
> washer or cartridge is a ten-minute job and usually the cheapest saving
> there is.

## Step 2: Cut the big, easy wins

- **Fix every leak** — taps, pipes, cisterns, hose fittings.
- **Shorter showers** or a bucket wash; turn the tap off while soaping,
  brushing teeth, or shaving.
- **Full loads only** in the washing machine.
- **A lid on the pot:** cooking with a lid and just enough water saves water
  and fuel at once.
- **Water the garden at dawn or dusk**, at the roots, under mulch — never at
  midday, when much of it evaporates.
- **Flush less**, or not at all: see [[survival-dry-toilet]].

## Step 3: Use each litre twice — the reuse cascade

Water gets a little dirtier at each step. Let it flow *down* the cascade,
from cleanest use to roughest, before it leaves the house.

```ascii
   CLEAN  drinking & cooking (treated water only)
     |
     v    rinse water from veg, pasta, beans
   wash   hands, bodies, dishes, laundry
     |
     v    collect in a bowl or bucket
   rinse  second rinse of clothes or floors
     |
     v
   rough  flush the toilet, or water trees
          and shrubs (same day, under mulch)
     |
     v
   drain / soil
```

*Fig. 1: the reuse cascade — each step uses water that the step above has
already used once, so the cleanest water is saved for drinking*

- **Catch the cold water** that runs while you wait for the shower to warm
  up — it's perfectly clean; use it for cooking, kettles, or plants.
- **Save vegetable-rinsing water** in a bowl for houseplants or the garden.
- **Wash in a bowl, not under a running tap**, then use the bowl water to
  flush the toilet (pour it straight into the pan) or for trees.
- **Never store used water** for more than a day; see
  [[water-greywater-reuse]] for the rules on what can go on the garden.

## Step 4: Plan a fair share when supply is short

When a street, building, or group shares one supply — a well, a tank, a
water tanker — fairness matters as much as quantity. People cooperate when
they can see the rules are fair and the same for everyone.

1. **Agree it together, early.** Decide the plan in a meeting while there's
   still water, not in an argument at an empty tank. See
   [[cooperation-run-a-meeting]].
2. **Measure, don't guess.** Know how much is in the tank and how much comes
   in each day, then divide.
3. **A base share for every person,** equal for all, covering drinking,
   cooking, and basic hygiene.
4. **Extra for need, agreed openly:** babies and young children, pregnant
   and breastfeeding people, the sick, older people, and anyone working hard
   in heat.
5. **Keep it visible:** a simple tally sheet by the tank, filled in by
   whoever draws water, and rotate who keeps it.
6. **Review often.** Meet again when the level drops, rain comes, or someone
   says the plan has stopped being fair.

> **Spec:** Humanitarian agencies plan on roughly **15 litres per person per
> day** as a bare minimum for drinking, cooking, and basic hygiene in a
> crisis, of which around **3 litres is drinking water** — more in heat or
> for anyone ill or breastfeeding. Treat these as a floor, not a target.

## A household rationing plan

Write this down and stick it inside a cupboard door, so nobody has to work
it out under stress:

1. **Ring-fence drinking water** in its own sealed, labelled containers —
   at least 3 litres per person per day for as many days as you can store.
2. **Cooking next:** one-pot meals that use little water, and save the
   cooking water for soup or the garden.
3. **Hygiene:** handwashing with soap after the toilet and before food never
   gets cut. Swap showers for a bowl wash; use the bowl water to flush.
4. **Laundry and dishes:** fewer, fuller washes; scrape plates before
   washing.
5. **Garden:** reused water only, to the most valuable plants first.

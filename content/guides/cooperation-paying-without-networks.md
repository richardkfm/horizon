# Keep paying and trading when cards stop working

A card terminal is a small computer that asks a bank, over a network, whether
to approve each payment. No network, no answer, no sale — and the ATM on the
corner is the same machine in a thicker box. This is the part of an outage
people meet first and plan for least: the shop is open, the shelves are full,
and nobody in the queue can pay for anything.

For a short outage the fix is small and old-fashioned: a little cash, a
notebook, and an agreement to sort out the rest afterwards.

> **Tip:** Keep the cash float in **small notes and coins**. A shop with no
> terminal usually has no way to make change either, and the person holding
> the largest note is the one who goes home empty-handed.

## Before it happens

- **Keep a modest cash reserve at home**, enough for a few days of the things
  you actually buy — food, bread, fuel, a bus fare, the pharmacy. Not a
  hoard; a float.
- **Split it up**: some in the go-bag, some at home, a little in a coat
  pocket. One misplaced envelope shouldn't take all of it.
- **Write down what you owe and what you're owed** — rent, a standing
  arrangement with a neighbour, the money someone is holding for you. Your
  banking app is exactly as offline as everything else.
- **Refill it after you use it.** The most common failure is a float that was
  spent during the last outage and never replaced.

## In the shop, on the day

- **Ask before you fill the basket.** Terminals sometimes work while the
  shop's own internet is down, sometimes not; find out at the door rather
  than at the till.
- **Buy what you need, not what you fear.** Short outages end. A queue of
  people buying a fortnight's food is what turns a two-hour inconvenience
  into empty shelves for the neighbours behind them.
- **Offer exact change** where you can. It keeps the till working for
  everyone after you.
- **Fuel, pharmacies, and public transport** are the three that catch people
  out. A tank half full and a prescription collected on time cost nothing to
  arrange in advance.

## Running a stall, shop, or shared kitchen through it

Traders have done this for centuries; the paperwork is genuinely simple.

```ascii
   TAB CARD                          no. 14, Selma
   ------------------------------------------------
   date   what                    value    signed
   ------------------------------------------------
   Tue    2 loaves, 1 kg rice     4.80     S.M. / R.
   Wed    milk, 6 eggs            3.10     S.M. / R.
   ------------------------------------------------
   running total                  7.90
   to settle when the banks are back. Copy held by both.
```

*Fig. 1: a tab card — dated lines, normal prices, both parties' initials, and two copies so neither side is trusting memory*

- **Write it down at the moment of the sale**, not from memory at closing
  time. Date, what was taken, the value, and initials from both sides.
- **Make two copies.** The seller keeps one, the buyer keeps one. Nearly
  every argument afterwards is about a figure only one person wrote down.
- **Use your ordinary prices.** An outage is not a market opportunity.
  Charging more because people are stuck is exploitation, and the
  neighbourhood will remember it long after the network is back.
- **Say plainly when the tab is due** — "when the terminals are back", "end
  of the month" — and don't attach interest or a penalty to it.
- **Total it by hand.** *Work out a fair trade or barter value* covers
  costing goods when there's no price to fall back on, and *track a household
  or group's stores and supplies* covers keeping the stock book that goes
  with it.

## Don't let anyone go without

Someone in every street has no cash at all that week, and nothing to do about
it until the machines return.

- **Extend a tab rather than refusing a sale**, especially for food,
  medicines, and anything for a child.
- **Let neighbours cover for each other** — a small sum lent openly, written
  down, settled later, is how this has always worked.
- **Watch for the person too proud to ask.** Offer once, plainly, without an
  audience, and leave the door open.
- **Nobody signs away anything that matters.** A few days of groceries is
  never worth a piece of property, a tool someone needs for work, or an
  open-ended promise of labour. Refuse those deals on both sides of the
  counter, and say out loud that the street doesn't do them.

## When the network comes back

- **Settle the tabs within a few days**, before the details get fuzzy and the
  goodwill wears off.
- **Check your own statements.** Payments attempted during the outage can
  land late, twice, or not at all; a payment you thought failed may still
  have gone through.
- **Replace your cash float** and say thank you to whoever carried you.
- **Note what ran out.** The gaps — no small change, no cash at all, no list
  of who was owed what — are cheap to fix now and expensive to fix mid-queue.

# Protect someone from coercion or threats inside the group

Most friction between people is a dispute — two sides with different needs
(see resolve conflict between people fairly). This is different: one person
using intimidation, manipulation, coercion, or force to control or harm
another. Treating it as a two-sided disagreement to mediate can make it
worse, by putting the person at risk in a room with the person harming them
and asking them to "meet in the middle." This guide is about protecting
someone, not settling a dispute — recall or a role change (see set up fair,
accountable governance for a group) may follow, but safety always comes
first.

> **Principle:** The safety of the person at risk comes before any process.
> Boundary-setting is protective, not punitive — it isn't a verdict on who
> is "right," it's what keeps someone safe while the group figures out what
> comes next.

## Do now: if someone is in immediate danger

> **Do now:** get the person somewhere safe, away from whoever is causing
> harm. Don't arrange or encourage a face-to-face confrontation to "sort it
> out." If there's immediate danger, contact local emergency services or a
> domestic-violence/abuse helpline — this comes before any group process.

## Recognise coercive and controlling behaviour

Coercion is often gradual, which is exactly what makes it hard to name early.
Watch for:

- **Isolating someone** from friends, family, or the rest of the group, or
  controlling who they can talk to.
- **Controlling resources or information** — money, transport, food,
  communication — so the person has fewer real options.
- **Threats and intimidation**, including subtle ones ("no one would believe
  you") as much as overt ones.
- **Escalating small violations** — a boundary crossed a little, then a
  little more, testing what will be tolerated.

> **Risk:** The biggest danger is minimising early signs because no single
> incident looks serious enough on its own. Coercion is usually a pattern,
> not one event — take the pattern seriously even when each piece looks
> small.

## Centre the person at risk, not reconciliation

- **Believe them and let them set the pace.** Don't require proof before
  taking a concern seriously, and don't push for details before they're
  ready to share them.
- **Don't force a joint conversation.** Mediation assumes both sides are
  safe and willing (see the "avoid if" note in resolve conflict between
  people fairly) — that assumption doesn't hold here.
- **Ask what they need**, rather than deciding for them. Their agency is
  part of what coercion takes away; don't take it away again by acting
  without their consent, except where someone is in immediate danger.

## Set and enforce boundaries

- **Name the specific behaviour**, plainly and without exaggeration — what
  happened, and what it did.
- **Use the group's existing accountability tools.** A role someone holds
  can be paused or ended through recall (see set up fair, accountable
  governance for a group) regardless of whether a full removal from the
  group is also needed.
- **Limit contact** where full removal isn't (yet) warranted — separating
  shared spaces, roles, or shifts so the person at risk isn't required to
  interact with whoever is causing harm.

> **Pick this if:** the behaviour is serious but the person at risk wants to
> stay in the group and isn't asking for a full removal — limited contact
> and a supervised, opt-in path back to shared spaces can work.

> **Avoid if:** the harm is severe, repeated, or the person at risk doesn't
> feel safe with any continued contact — full removal from group spaces and
> roles is the fair outcome, not a partial measure that keeps asking them to
> manage the risk themselves.

## Build a safety plan

- **Agree on safe people and places** the person at risk can go to without
  explanation.
- **Set up a check-in buddy** — someone who knows to notice if contact goes
  quiet.
- **Document incidents** as they happen: date, what happened, who else was
  present. This supports the group's accountability process later, even if
  nothing is decided yet.
- **Get consent before sharing** their situation further within the group —
  it's their story to tell, on their terms, except where immediate safety
  requires otherwise.

## When to involve people outside the group

The group is not always the right or only place to resolve this. Offer,
rather than mandate:

- **Domestic-violence or abuse helplines and services**, which exist
  specifically for this and know more than any group can improvise.
- **Independent mediation or restorative-justice services**, for cases that
  don't need the criminal-justice system but do need a trained facilitator.
- **Law enforcement**, if the person at risk chooses it. It is their choice
  to make, not the group's to make for them — respecting that is part of
  not recreating the same coercive dynamic in a different form.

## Support afterward

Fear and shock don't end when the immediate danger does. See support
someone through shock, trauma, or grief for how to help someone, or
yourself, through the aftermath.

# Keep a neighbourhood informed without the internet

When the group chat stops, most neighbourhoods discover they have no other
way of talking to themselves. What replaces it needs no technology: one place
where written information lives, a handful of people who carry it to the
doors that can't reach it, and an agreement about how messages are written.
Set up on a quiet afternoon, it costs nothing and works for the street party
as readily as for an outage.

> **Note:** This is a system for sharing information by consent, not for
> keeping track of anybody. Nobody has to join, nobody's movements get
> recorded, and what goes on a public board is only ever what its subject is
> happy to have read by strangers.

## The board is the single source of truth

Pick one place everybody already walks past — a shop window, a block's
entrance hall, a lamp post at the corner, a fence by the bus stop. Put up a
weatherproof sheet or a board, in a bag or under a plastic sleeve if the
weather is bad, with a pen on a string.

```ascii
   +---------------------------------------------+
   |  STREET NOTICES       checked: Tue 14:00    |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+
   |  CONFIRMED  | water on again from 11:00     |
   |             |   - regional radio, Tue 10:40 |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+
   |  OFFERED    | spare freezer space, no. 14   |
   |             | lift to the clinic, Thu am    |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+
   |  NEEDED     | anyone with a corded phone?   |
   |             | help carrying water to no. 3  |
   +-------------+-------------------------------+
   |  next check of this board: Tue 18:00        |
   +---------------------------------------------+
```

*Fig. 1: three columns and two timestamps — confirmed facts kept apart from offers and needs, so nobody has to guess which is which*

- **Confirmed, offered, needed.** Keeping facts in their own column stops the
  board turning into a rumour wall. Each confirmed item carries who said it
  and when — see *get trustworthy news when the internet is down*.
- **Every note is timestamped and signed** with a name and door number. An
  unsigned note can't be checked or corrected.
- **Say when the board is next updated.** People come back if they know when
  to; they stop reading it if it looks stale.
- **Take things down when they're done.** A board of old notices teaches
  people to ignore it.

## The relay carries it to the doors

A board only serves people who can walk to it. The relay is a handful of
neighbours who each look after a few doors — the smallest, dullest structure
that works, and the one that has always worked.

```ascii
      [ board ]
          |
     +----+----+----------+
     |         |          |
  nos. 1-8  nos. 9-16  nos. 17-24     each person: a few doors,
     |         |          |            a knock twice a day, and
  knock     knock      knock           a note back to the board
```

*Fig. 2: a few doors each, not one person doing the whole street*

- **Volunteer for a handful of doors, not a hundred.** Six to ten is a ten
  minute walk; a whole street is a job nobody sustains past day two.
- **Agree two rounds a day** — morning and early evening — so people know
  when to expect a knock and don't sit waiting.
- **Carry information both ways.** The relay isn't a broadcast: what comes
  back from the doors (someone's out of medicine, someone has a generator to
  lend) is the half that makes it worth doing.
- **Ask before you include someone.** Some people want checking on; some
  emphatically don't. Both answers are fine and neither is permanent — ask
  again next time.
- **Never list a vulnerability on a public board.** "No. 12 is alone and
  frail" belongs in the head of the person who knocks there, not in a window.

## Reach further than walking

- **Runners** cover the distance between neighbourhoods. Send the message in
  writing, with the time on it, and have it initialled at the far end so
  everyone knows it arrived.
- **Scheduled radio check-ins** beat continuous listening: agree a time and a
  channel, keep it short, and let everyone switch off between. See *set up
  two-way radio for your community*.
- **Pass files by hand.** A USB stick walked down the road moves more than
  any radio link — documents, photographs of a notice, a copy of this
  horizon node's guides.
- **Local network, if you want messaging back.** *Build a local network
  without the internet* and *build a resilient mesh network* cover getting
  neighbours typing to each other again with no uplink at all. That is a
  weekend's work, so for a short outage start with the board.

## Make sure it reaches everyone

- **People who can't get to the board**: the relay is for them first —
  anyone housebound, unwell, without a carer that week.
- **Deaf, blind, and partially sighted neighbours**: big print, a written
  copy handed over for those who don't want to be read to, and a knock that
  waits. See *communicate with signs, gestures, and pictograms*.
- **Neighbours whose first language isn't yours**: a second copy of the key
  notices in the languages of your street — see *communicate across a
  language barrier*.
- **Children and teenagers** make excellent runners and are usually delighted
  to be asked, within sight of home and in daylight.

## Stand it down properly

When the connection comes back, take the notices down, put a last note up
saying so and thanking people, and write down who covered which doors. The
list is the whole setup cost of doing it again — and the next time, whether
it's an outage or a heatwave or a burst main, the street starts from a
working relay instead of from nothing.

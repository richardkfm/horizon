# Store a harvest in a root cellar or cold store

Before fridges, every household kept its autumn harvest through the winter in
a cellar, a clamp, or a cold back room. It still works beautifully: potatoes,
carrots, beetroot, cabbages, apples, onions, and squash can stay fresh for
months with **no power at all**. It's a way to eat your own (or a local
farm's) food long after the season, and to buy in bulk when it's cheap.

> **Note:** Cold storage keeps *fresh, whole, healthy* produce. It doesn't
> make bruised or damaged food last — eat those first. For cooked food and
> leftovers, see [[cooking-food-safety-no-fridge]].

## Four zones, not one

Different crops want different conditions. Most homes have more than one
kind of cool space; match each crop to the right one.

```ascii
   ZONE          TEMP     AIR       GOOD FOR
   -----------   ------   -------   ------------------
   COLD & DAMP   0-4 C    very      carrots, beetroot,
   cellar floor,          damp      parsnips, turnips,
   clamp, bin                       cabbage, leeks

   COOL & DAMP   4-10 C   damp      potatoes (in the
                                    dark!)

   COLD, APART   0-4 C    damp,     apples, pears
   own corner             vented    (away from veg)

   COOL & DRY    0-10 C   dry,      onions, garlic,
   shed, porch            airy      shallots

   MILD & DRY    10-15 C  dry       squash, pumpkins,
   spare room                       sweet potatoes,
                                    dried beans, grain
```

*Fig. 1: storage zones — roots like it cold and damp, onions cool and dry,
squash mild and dry; apples go in their own corner*

- **Damp** means about 90–95% humidity — the air feels moist and roots don't
  shrivel. A floor of damp sand or gravel, or damp sacking, helps.
- **Dry** means about 60–70% — onions and squash rot in damp air.
- **Potatoes** turn sweet below about 4 °C and go green and bitter in light.
  Keep them cool, but not freezing, and in the dark.
- **Nothing should freeze.** Frozen roots and apples turn to mush when they
  thaw.

> **Tip:** Hang a cheap thermometer and a humidity meter (hygrometer) in each
> space for a few weeks before harvest. You'll quickly learn which corner is
> which.

## Keep ethylene producers apart

Some fruits give off **ethylene**, a natural gas that ripens and ages
everything around them.

- **Producers:** apples, pears, ripe tomatoes, bananas.
- **Sensitive to it:** potatoes sprout, carrots turn bitter, cabbages yellow,
  leafy greens wilt.
- **So:** keep apples and pears in a separate, well-vented room or at the far
  end from vegetables, or wrap each apple in newspaper.
- **Cabbages and turnips smell strong** and can taint apples — another reason
  to keep fruit apart.

## Prepare crops for storage

1. **Harvest at full maturity** in dry weather, late in the season.
2. **Handle gently** — every bruise is where rot starts.
3. **Don't wash roots.** Brush off loose soil and let them dry. Twist off
   carrot and beetroot tops, leaving a short stub.
4. **Cure the ones that need it:**
   - **Potatoes:** 1–2 weeks somewhere dark and mild to toughen the skin.
   - **Onions and garlic:** dry in the sun or an airy shed until the necks
     are papery and fully dry.
   - **Winter squash and pumpkins:** 1–2 weeks somewhere warm and sunny
     until the skin is too hard to dent with a fingernail.
5. **Store only perfect specimens.** Eat damaged ones now.

## Set up your store

- **A basement corner or cellar** on an outside wall, insulated from the
  heated part of the house, is ideal.
- **A clamp:** a mound of roots on a bed of straw in the garden, covered with
  a thick layer of straw then soil, with a straw "chimney" for air. Open one
  end at a time to take what you need.
- **A buried bin:** a lidded metal or plastic bin sunk into the ground, roots
  layered in damp sand, covered with straw and a board.
- **An unheated room, porch, or garage** for onions, squash, and apples.
- **Ventilation:** cold air in low, warm air out high. Open vents on cold
  nights, close them on warm days and in hard frost.
- **Rodent-proof it** with fine wire mesh over vents and around bins, so
  mice and rats can't get in — no traps or poison needed.

> **Pick this if:** you grow more roots than you can eat by Christmas. Pack
> carrots, beetroot, and parsnips in boxes of slightly damp sand, sawdust, or
> leaf mould, not touching each other — they'll keep crisp into spring.

## Check for rot

- **Check every week.** Look, sniff, and gently squeeze.
- **Remove anything soft, mouldy, sprouting, or smelly** straight away —
  rot spreads from one item to its neighbours. "One bad apple" is true.
- **Use the most fragile first:** cabbages and pears before potatoes; carrots
  before parsnips.
- **Watch the conditions:** shrivelling means too dry — dampen the floor or
  sand. Mould everywhere means too damp or too warm — open the vents.
- **Clean out the store** completely before the next harvest.

## How long things keep

Rough times in good conditions — yours may vary:

| Crop | Keeps for |
| --- | --- |
| Carrots, beetroot, parsnips (in damp sand) | 4–6 months |
| Potatoes | 4–6 months |
| Late cabbage | 2–4 months |
| Apples (late-keeping varieties) | 2–5 months |
| Onions, garlic | 4–8 months |
| Winter squash, pumpkins | 3–6 months |

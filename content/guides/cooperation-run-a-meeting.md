# Run a good meeting

Most people have sat through a bad meeting: no clear purpose, the same few
voices, nothing decided, and it overruns. A good meeting is a skill anyone
can learn, and it's the everyday engine of a fair group — a residents'
association, a food co-op, a garden, a street getting through a hard winter.
When people leave feeling heard and knowing what happens next, they come back.

Good meetings rest on one idea: **genuine consent, not a headcount**. A
decision is only as strong as the people who'll carry it out, and a minority
that's been steamrolled stops cooperating. Taking the time to hear the
quietest person in the room is how a group stays fair — and how it finds the
idea nobody else had.

> **Note:** This guide is the practical "how" of a single meeting. For the
> bigger picture — how a group decides by consent and stays accountable over
> time — see [[cooperation-group-decisions]] and
> [[cooperation-democratic-governance]].

## Before the meeting

1. **Write an agenda** and share it a few days ahead, so people can think and
   so anyone can add an item.
2. **Mark each item** as *information*, *discussion*, or *decision*, with a
   rough time for each. Put decisions early, while people are fresh.
3. **Circulate any proposal in writing** beforehand — who's proposing it,
   what exactly, why, and what it will cost in time or resources.
4. **Choose an accessible place and time:** step-free, seats, light, warmth,
   and a time carers and workers can make. Offer a way to join by phone.

## Share out the roles

Rotate roles every meeting, so skills spread and nobody becomes "the boss".

- **Facilitator:** keeps to the agenda, makes sure everyone is heard, and
  stays neutral on the content. If they want to argue a point, they hand the
  role over for that item.
- **Note-taker:** records decisions, actions, and any objections — not every
  word.
- **Timekeeper:** gives gentle warnings ("five minutes left on this item")
  and helps the group decide whether to extend or move on.
- **Optional — a "mood watcher":** notices who hasn't spoken, tension, or
  tiredness, and suggests a break or a go-round.

## Open the meeting

1. **A quick go-round:** names, and one sentence each — how you are, or what
   you hope to get out of today. It gets every voice into the room early.
2. **Agree the agenda and the finish time.**
3. **Remind everyone of a few agreements:** one person speaks at a time; keep
   it short; challenge ideas, not people; anyone can ask for a pause.

## Hear quiet voices first

Open discussion favours the confident and the fast. Use structure instead.

```ascii
          (start with whoever has
           spoken least today)
                   1
             8           2
          7     [ topic ]   3      each person speaks once,
             6           4         or says "pass";
                   5               nobody speaks twice until
                                   everyone has had a turn
```

*Fig. 1: a go-round — going round the circle in order, starting from the
quieter side, gives every person one uninterrupted turn*

- **Go-rounds:** everyone speaks in turn, once, or passes. Start with people
  who have spoken least, not with whoever has their hand up.
- **A "stack":** the facilitator keeps a list of people who want to speak and
  moves first-time speakers up the list.
- **Pairs first:** two minutes discussing in pairs before an open round helps
  people who need time to think.
- **Ask directly but gently:** "We haven't heard from this side of the room
  yet — anyone want to add something?"

## From proposal to decision

1. **Present the proposal** clearly — ideally in writing, read out.
2. **Clarifying questions only** — "what does this mean?" — not opinions yet.
3. **Reactions in a go-round:** what people like, and any concerns.
4. **Amend:** look for changes that meet the concerns raised. Often the
   proposal gets much better here.
5. **Test for consent:** read the final wording, then ask each person — not
   "who's in favour?" but "can you live with this?" Each person answers
   with one of the options below.

| Response | Means | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Consent** | "I can live with this and will help carry it out." | Counts towards agreement |
| **Stand aside** | "I disagree, but I won't stop the group." | Recorded by name in the minutes; the decision goes ahead |
| **Block** | "This would seriously harm the group or break our core values." | The proposal doesn't pass as it stands; the group works on it further |

> **Decision:** A block is rare and serious — it's for a principled objection
> about the whole group, not personal preference. If a block comes up, don't
> pressure the person to drop it. Ask what would need to change, and give the
> concern real time, perhaps at a follow-up meeting or in a small working
> group that includes the person who blocked.

> **Risk:** Several stand-asides on one decision is a warning sign: people
> are going along without really agreeing. Pause and ask whether the proposal
> needs more work.

## If you can't agree today

- **Park it.** It's fine to say "not yet" — a rushed decision is worse than a
  late one.
- **Send it to a small working group** that includes people with different
  views, to bring back a new proposal.
- **Try a trial period:** agree to try something for a month, then review.
- **Keep a fallback** for genuinely urgent matters, agreed in advance by the
  whole group — not invented in the moment by whoever is loudest. See
  [[cooperation-resolving-conflict]] if the disagreement is between people
  rather than ideas.

## Record decisions and actions

The note-taker writes, and reads back before the meeting ends:

- **Each decision,** in the exact words agreed, with any stand-asides.
- **Each action:** *who* does *what*, by *when*.
- **Items parked** for next time.

Share the minutes within a day or two, somewhere everyone can read them —
a notice board, a shared folder, a printed copy.

## End on time

- **Finish when you said you would.** If more time is needed, ask the whole
  group, don't just carry on.
- **A closing go-round:** one word each on how it went.
- **Set the next date** and roles.
- **Thank people** — for coming, for the work, and for the disagreement that
  improved the decision.

# Coordinate community security against outside threats

This is about an outside individual or small group threatening a
neighbourhood or community with force at everyday scale — harassment,
hate-motivated targeting, or opportunistic violence — not armed conflict or
war. If fighting has reached where you live, see keep your household safe
during armed conflict instead; this guide is for the more common, smaller,
and often ongoing situation of a hostile actor rather than a conflict zone.

> **This is protective, not vigilante.** No weapons, no armed patrols, no
> confronting the threat yourself. The aim is to notice early, coordinate,
> de-escalate, and disengage — not to fight anyone.

## Do now: if someone is in immediate danger

> **Do now:** call local emergency services, get the person somewhere safe,
> and don't engage the threat directly. Coordination and watching out for
> each other matter most *before* an incident — in the moment, safety comes
> from distance and outside help, not confrontation.

## Build a communication network before you need it

- **Set up a phone tree or group chat** so information about a concern
  reaches everyone quickly, not just whoever happened to see it.
- **Consider two-way radio or a local mesh network** for when phone
  networks are down or unreliable — see set up two-way radio for your
  community and build a local network without the internet. Keep this to
  the same everyday, plain-language coordination those guides describe;
  this is neighbours staying in touch, not tactical communications.
- **Agree a known point of contact** and a simple way to raise a concern, so
  a real worry doesn't start with "who do we even tell?"

## Organise presence, not patrols

- **Improve shared-space lighting** around entrances, paths, and parking —
  visibility deters more than confrontation does.
- **Use a buddy system after dark** rather than people moving around alone.
- **Run a watch rota that notices and alerts, never confronts.** Its whole
  job is to see something and tell someone — not to detain, chase, or
  challenge anyone.

> **Pick this if:** you want people looking out for each other and passing
> on what they notice — that's a community watch, and it's the right tool
> here.

> **Avoid if:** what's being proposed looks like an armed patrol, a
> militia, or anything meant to confront or deter by force. That isn't a
> stricter version of a community watch — it's a different, unacceptable
> thing, and horizon doesn't cover it because it puts people in more danger,
> not less.

## Protect targeted and vulnerable members

Hate-motivated or ideologically-driven targeting often falls on the same
people repeatedly.

- **Centre whoever is targeted** without requiring them to prove the threat
  is real before the group takes it seriously.
- **Offer practical support** — someone to walk with, varying routines or
  routes, a place to go if home doesn't feel safe.
- **Connect with identity-specific advocacy or support organisations**
  where they exist; they often know the specific risks and resources better
  than an improvised group response can.

## De-escalate and disengage

- **Lower your voice and slow down** rather than matching an aggressor's
  energy; most confrontations de-escalate faster when one side isn't
  escalating back.
- **Create distance and an exit**, don't block one — your own escape route
  matters as much as anyone else's.
- **Avoid crowds forming around a confrontation** — a crowd tends to
  escalate a situation, not calm it.
- **Document afterward**, not during: time, description, what was said or
  done. This supports a report without putting anyone at risk gathering it
  in the moment.

## Work with outside authorities and networks

- **Report threats and hate incidents** to police or a relevant civil-rights
  hotline where you trust them to act on it.
- **Lean on ties with neighbouring groups** (see build trust and agreements
  with neighbouring groups) — a threat to one household is often a shared
  concern, and a wider network notices more and responds faster than one
  household alone.

## Support afterward

A frightening incident leaves more than physical harm behind. See support
someone through shock, trauma, or grief for how to help someone, or
yourself, through the aftermath.

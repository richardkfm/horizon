# Keep the information you depend on offline

Very little of what we call "having information" is actually on the device in
our hand. Contacts, documents, tickets, manuals, recipes, medical records and
login codes mostly live somewhere else and are fetched on demand. That is
efficient and completely fine — right up until the fetching stops, which is
what an outage is. One afternoon of copying turns the next outage from a
problem into a minor annoyance.

> **Tip:** This is worth doing for ordinary life, not just emergencies. A
> train with no signal, a holiday cottage, a foreign SIM, a flat battery on
> someone else's phone — the same saved copies rescue all of them.

## Three layers, in order of how well they survive

```ascii
  paper          | contacts, addresses, key documents, frequencies,
  no power       | account numbers, the meeting point, medicines
  no device      |   -> small, dull, survives everything
  ---------------+---------------------------------------------------
  your device    | offline maps, saved documents, downloaded manuals,
  needs charge   | photos of papers, an offline reference app
                 |   -> everything you need alone, in your pocket
  ---------------+---------------------------------------------------
  a local node   | a whole offline library, map packs, guides for the
  needs power    | household or street to share
                 |   -> the deep reference nobody can carry
```

*Fig. 1: each layer holds less and survives more — build downward from paper, not upward from the cloud*

Don't try to get everything onto every layer. Put the few things you'd be
lost without on paper, the things you'd want on hand on your device, and the
bulk reference on a node someone runs.

## Start with the paper layer

Half an hour with a pen beats any amount of software here.

- **Phone numbers and addresses** for the people you would actually contact:
  family, a neighbour, your doctor, your landlord or a tradesperson, an
  out-of-area friend everyone can check in with. Nobody knows numbers by
  heart any more, and a dead phone takes the whole address book with it.
- **Your household's plan**: where to meet if you're separated, who checks on
  whom, when to expect each other back.
- **Radio frequencies** for local and national news (see *get trustworthy
  news when the internet is down*).
- **Account and policy numbers**, your medicines and doses, allergies, and
  any condition a stranger treating you would need to know.
- Keep it in a sealed bag with the go-bag, and put a second copy somewhere
  else — a relative's house, your desk at work.

## Then the device layer

- **Download your maps** for home, work, and anywhere you would travel — see
  *use offline maps and GPS with no signal*.
- **Save documents for offline use**, don't just bookmark them. In most cloud
  storage apps there is a "make available offline", "pin", or download
  option. A file you can only *see* in a list is not on your phone.
- **Photograph your papers**: passport, ID, insurance, prescriptions, the
  serial numbers of anything you'd claim for. Keep the photos in a folder
  that is saved offline, not only synced.
- **Keep the manuals** for your boiler, stove, water pump, and car. These are
  exactly what you need during an outage and exactly what you'd normally
  search for.
- **Install one offline reference** — an encyclopaedia reader, a first-aid
  app, a plant guide — and check it genuinely works with the network off.

> **Risk:** Two-factor login is the trap that catches everyone. Codes sent
> by SMS never arrive with the network down, and a password manager that
> hasn't synced offline can lock you out of your own accounts on the day you
> need them. Print your **recovery codes** now, keep them with the paper
> layer, make sure your password vault is downloaded to the device, and
> prefer an authenticator app — which generates codes offline from the clock
> — over SMS codes wherever a service offers the choice.

## The node layer: a shared offline library

A single low-power machine in the house or on the street can hold far more
than any phone, and everyone connects to it over local wifi with no internet
involved at all.

- **This horizon node is already one.** Its guides and checklists work with
  nothing else running. If whoever operates it has installed content packs
  (**Admin → Content packs**), there may also be an offline encyclopaedia
  under **Reference** and offline maps under **Maps** — worth asking them
  what's on it *before* you need it.
- **Turn an old PC into a local server** covers building one from a laptop
  that is too slow for anything else.
- **Build a local network without the internet** is how the rest of the
  household or street reaches it.

## Keep it honest with one drill

An archive nobody has tested is a guess. Twice a year — same day as the smoke
alarms and the radio batteries:

1. Put your phone in airplane mode with wifi off.
2. Open the map of your area, one saved document, your password vault, and
   the manual for your heating. Anything that spins, asks you to sign in, or
   shows a blank page is not actually saved.
3. Check the paper copy still matches reality — numbers change, people move,
   prescriptions get updated.
4. Refresh anything stale, and copy the device layer to a USB stick kept
   somewhere else in the building.

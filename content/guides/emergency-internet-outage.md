# Cope when the internet and phone networks go down

Connections drop more often than people expect: a cut cable, a storm, a
failed exchange, an overloaded mobile mast, a provider's bad software update,
or a deliberate shutdown. Almost all of these last hours to a few days and
then end. The discomfort is real — no maps, no messages, no card payments —
but it is temporary, and a household that knows what still works handles it
calmly instead of anxiously.

> **Do now:** If someone needs an ambulance, fire service, or police and no
> phone works, send someone in person to the nearest staffed station or to a
> neighbour with a working line. Try an emergency call anyway first — those
> numbers can route over *any* operator's network, so a call may connect even
> when your own provider is dead.

## First, find out how far it reaches

Most of what you decide depends on whether this is your flat or your region.
Work down the chain until something answers.

```ascii
   you           your home        your street      your provider   everywhere
 [ phone ] -wifi-> [ router ] -line-> [ cabinet ] -> [ ISP ] -> [ wider net ]
     |                 |                  |             |             |
 mobile data       lights red        neighbours     provider      radio says
 still works?      or all dark       also down?     at fault?     it's regional
     |                 |                  |             |             |
  -> your home      -> your kit        -> a local     -> nothing    -> settle in
     line only         only               fault          to fix        calmly
```

*Fig. 1: work outward from your own kit — each step you rule out tells you how long to expect it to last and who can fix it*

- **Turn mobile data on and off wifi.** If pages load, only your home line is
  down. If neither works, the problem is bigger than your house.
- **Look at the router.** All lights dark means a power problem (see *prepare
  for and live through a long blackout*). A red or missing internet light
  means the line, not your device.
- **Power-cycle once.** Unplug the router for 30 seconds, plug it back in,
  and give it a full two minutes to come back. Do this *once*. Repeatedly
  restarting fixes nothing, and never press the recessed factory-reset pin —
  you will lose your settings and still have no connection.
- **Ask a neighbour.** Two households with different providers both down
  means a street or area fault. This is also the moment you find out who on
  the street has a radio, a landline, or a car with a working aerial.
- **Check the radio.** A battery or car radio tells you within minutes
  whether this is regional news or just your building. See *get trustworthy
  news when the internet is down*.

## What still works

| Still works | Why | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GPS on your phone | Satellites, no network needed | Navigation, *if* maps were downloaded first |
| FM/AM radio | Broadcast, one-way | News, official instructions |
| Emergency alerts | Cell broadcast, needs no data | Official warnings, often even with no data |
| SMS | Tiny messages squeeze through congestion | Short check-ins when calls fail |
| Analogue landline | Powered down the phone line itself | Calls, if you still have a corded phone |
| Two-way radio | Independent of all infrastructure | Coordinating with neighbours |
| Anything downloaded | Already on the device | Maps, documents, music, this horizon node |
| Cash | No terminal, no network | Shops, market stalls, fuel |

> **Note:** A cordless handset and a "phone" socket on a fibre or cable
> router both need mains power and a working provider — they are not the
> line-powered landline above. A cheap corded phone kept in a drawer is what
> actually keeps ringing.

## The first hour

1. **Save battery now, not later.** Low-power mode, screen dim, close apps
   that hunt for a signal. A phone searching for a lost network drains fast;
   airplane mode with wifi off stops the hunting, and you can switch it back
   on to check every so often.
2. **Send one short message, not five calls.** If texts are getting through,
   one SMS saying *"we're fine, at home"* beats repeated redialling, which
   only adds to the congestion everyone is stuck in.
3. **Agree a fallback with your household** before anyone leaves the house:
   where to meet, roughly when to be back, and who checks on whom. This is
   the single thing people wish they had done.
4. **Don't panic-buy.** Short outages end. Stripping a shop shelf hurts the
   neighbours who arrive an hour later, and a queue of people buying a week's
   food with cards that don't work makes the checkout worse for everyone.

## Check on the people it hits hardest

An outage is an inconvenience for most people and a genuine problem for a
few. Knock on doors, especially if it runs past a few hours.

- **Anyone with a medical or care alarm** — those pendants and boxes usually
  dial out over a phone line or mobile network and go silent with it. Agree
  to check in person, on a schedule, until it is back.
- **People living alone, older neighbours, and anyone unwell.** Their usual
  daily contact may be the phone you have just lost.
- **Anyone who depends on a powered medical device** — see *keep essential
  medical care going without power* if the electricity is out too.
- **Households whose door entry, heating, or lights run through an app.**
  Find out where the manual override is before dark.

## Things that quietly stop working

Expect these, so they don't surprise you mid-errand: card terminals and
ATMs (see *keep paying and trading when cards stop working*), fuel pumps,
public transport ticketing and departure screens, smart locks and video
doorbells, cloud thermostats, streaming and smart TVs, work and school
systems, and any "two-factor" login that texts you a code. Your downloaded
music, your offline maps, your books, and this horizon node all carry on.

## If it runs past a day

Short outages are the common case, but if this one stretches on, the rest of
the library takes over:

- *Get trustworthy news when the internet is down* — receiving reliable
  information and not amplifying rumours.
- *Keep a neighbourhood informed without the internet* — a notice board and
  a relay that works with no technology at all.
- *Set up two-way radio for your community* — coordinating beyond shouting
  distance.
- *Build a local network without the internet* — if you want messaging and
  shared files back within the neighbourhood itself.

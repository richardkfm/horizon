# Help someone choking or not breathing

When someone can't breathe, every second counts. The two situations here are
**choking** (something blocking the airway) and **cardiac arrest** (no breathing,
no response). Both need fast, simple action.

> **Do now:** If someone collapses and isn't breathing normally, send for help and
> a defibrillator (AED), and start chest compressions: push hard and fast in the
> centre of the chest.

> **Warning:** First aid only. Get professional help immediately — phone emergency
> services if you possibly can, or send someone to fetch help while you act. If you
> can take a hands-on first-aid course, do; practice makes this far easier.

## Choking — when they can still cough

If the person can cough, speak, or breathe, **encourage them to keep coughing** —
coughing is the most effective way to clear a blockage. Stay with them.

## Choking — adult or child who can't breathe, cough, or speak

1. **Give up to 5 back blows.** Lean them forward and strike firmly between the
   shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
2. **Give up to 5 abdominal thrusts.** Stand behind, make a fist just above the
   navel, grasp it with your other hand, and pull sharply inwards and upwards.
3. **Alternate** 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out or the
   person becomes unresponsive.

> **Note:** For someone **pregnant** or too large to reach around, use **chest
> thrusts** instead of abdominal thrusts: stand behind, put your arms under
> their armpits, place your fist on the centre of the breastbone, grasp it with
> your other hand, and pull sharply straight back.

Anyone who has had abdominal or chest thrusts should be checked by a health
worker afterwards, even if they feel fine.

## Choking — baby under one year

**Never use abdominal thrusts on a baby.**

1. **5 back blows:** sit or kneel, lay the baby face down along your forearm
   with the head lower than the body, supporting the head by the jaw (keep your
   fingers off the throat). Give up to 5 firm blows between the shoulder blades
   with the heel of your other hand.
2. **5 chest thrusts:** turn the baby face up along your other arm, head still
   low. Put **two fingers** on the lower half of the breastbone, just below the
   nipple line, and push down sharply up to 5 times.
3. **Repeat** 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until the object comes out or the
   baby becomes unresponsive — then start baby CPR (below) and call for help.

Only pick an object out of the mouth if you can clearly see it; never sweep a
finger blindly.

## If they become unresponsive — start CPR

1. **Check response and breathing.** Tap them, shout. Tilt the head back, lift
   the chin, and look and listen for up to 10 seconds. If there's no normal
   breathing (occasional gasps don't count), begin CPR.
2. **Send for help** — get someone to call emergency services and **bring a
   defibrillator (AED)** if one exists nearby. If you're alone with a phone,
   put it on speaker and start.
3. **Chest compressions:** heel of your hand in the centre of the chest (the
   lower half of the breastbone), other hand on top, arms straight. Push down
   hard about 5–6 cm, then let the chest rise fully. Aim for about 100–120
   pushes a minute.
4. **Rescue breaths (if trained and willing):** after 30 compressions, give 2
   breaths, tilting the head back and lifting the chin. If you can't or won't,
   **keep doing continuous compressions** — that alone saves lives.
5. **Keep going** without stopping until help arrives, the AED tells you to
   pause, or the person starts breathing normally.

## Use a defibrillator (AED) as soon as one arrives

An AED is made for untrained people — it talks you through everything.

1. **Switch it on and follow its voice prompts.**
2. Bare the chest and stick the pads where the pictures show.
3. **Nobody touches the person** while it analyses or when it says to shock.
4. Restart compressions straight away when it tells you to, and leave the pads
   on until help takes over.

For a child, use child pads or the child setting if the AED has them;
otherwise adult pads are fine — for a small child, one on the front of the
chest and one on the back, so they don't touch.

## CPR for babies and children

The steps are the same; what changes is how you push and how hard.

| | Baby (under 1 year) | Child (1 year to puberty) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Push with** | Two fingertips on the lower half of the breastbone | The heel of one hand (two hands if needed) |
| **How deep** | About one-third of the chest depth (about 4 cm) | About one-third of the chest depth (about 5 cm) |
| **Rate** | 100–120 a minute | 100–120 a minute |
| **Head** | Level ("neutral"), not tilted far back | Tilted back, chin lifted |

- **If you're trained, start with 5 rescue breaths** (European Resuscitation
  Council guidance for children), because a child's arrest usually starts with
  a breathing problem. For a baby, cover the mouth *and* nose with your mouth.
- Then continue with **30 compressions to 2 breaths** (or 15 to 2 if you've been
  trained in children's life support).
- If you're not trained or can't give breaths, **compressions alone are far
  better than nothing** — start and keep going.
- If you're alone, give about a minute of CPR before leaving to call for help,
  unless you can call on speaker while you work.

## After

If they recover and are breathing normally, place them on their side (recovery
position), keep them warm, and stay with them until help arrives. Keep checking
their breathing; if it stops, start CPR again.

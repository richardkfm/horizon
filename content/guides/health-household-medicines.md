# Manage your household's medicines

Most households have a drawer of half-used packets, someone on a daily tablet,
and nobody quite sure what's in date. A little order goes a long way: a
written list for each person, a safe cool place to keep things, and a habit
of refilling *before* you run out. It makes ordinary life easier, and it means
you're not caught short when a pharmacy is closed or a trip runs long.

> **Risk:** This guide is about organising medicines, not choosing them.
> Never start, stop, or change a prescription medicine without talking to the
> prescriber or a pharmacist — stopping some medicines suddenly is dangerous.

## Step 1: Write a list for each person

One page per person, kept with the medicines and a copy in your go-bag or
phone. Paper still works when the power doesn't.

- **Name, date of birth,** and any **allergies** (and what happens).
- **Each medicine:** its name, strength, how much, when, and what it's for.
- **Over-the-counter medicines and supplements** too — they can interact.
- **Conditions** (diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, heart problems).
- **Prescriber and pharmacy contact details.**
- **Vaccination dates,** including the last tetanus booster.
- **Medical equipment** that needs power or supplies (see
  [[health-power-dependent-care]]).

Use the [[checklist:household-medicines]] checklist to make sure nothing's
missed.

> **Tip:** Use one pharmacy where you can. The pharmacist then sees everything
> a person takes and can spot a dangerous combination.

## Step 2: Store them safely

- **Cool and dry:** a cupboard away from the cooker and radiators. The
  bathroom is the worst place — steam and heat spoil tablets.
- **Out of children's reach and sight**, ideally locked. Many adult medicines
  — even iron tablets and paracetamol — can seriously harm a child.
- **In their original packaging**, with the label and leaflet, so you always
  know what and how much.
- **Fridge medicines** (some insulins, some eye drops and liquid antibiotics):
  in the main body of the fridge, never frozen and not against the back wall.

```ascii
   +-------------------------------------------+
   |  HIGH SHELF / LOCKED BOX  (out of reach)  |
   |   adult tablets    |  sharp / dangerous   |
   |-------------------------------------------|
   |  everyday shelf: first-aid kit, plasters  |
   +-------------------------------------------+
   cool, dry cupboard - NOT bathroom, NOT by cooker

   per person: [ list ] [ meds ] [ 7-day box ]
```

## Step 3: Understand expiry dates

The expiry date is the last date the maker guarantees the medicine is fully
safe and works fully, when stored as the label says. Past it, things vary:

- **Many dry tablets and capsules** slowly lose strength rather than becoming
  harmful — but you can't tell how much is left by looking.
- **Liquids, creams, eye drops, and inhalers** are more fragile. Eye drops
  usually have a short "use within" period once opened — check the label.
- **Insulin** is fragile: keep unopened insulin cold, never freeze it, and
  keep the one in use away from heat and sunlight. Once opened, most kinds
  last only a few weeks — check the leaflet.
- **Emergency medicines** (adrenaline auto-injectors, asthma relievers, heart
  sprays) must be in date — replace them before they expire.

> **Avoid if:** a medicine is past its date and is a **liquid antibiotic**,
> **insulin**, an **emergency medicine**, or looks, smells, or feels changed
> (crumbling, discoloured, cloudy, sticky). Don't use it — get a replacement.
> For anything else past its date, ask a pharmacist before relying on it.

## Step 4: Plan refills ahead

- **Count days, not packets.** Note on each person's list how many days are
  left and when to reorder.
- **Reorder with a week or two in hand** — more before holidays, long trips,
  or bad-weather seasons.
- **Ask the prescriber** whether a longer prescription or a small reserve is
  possible for essential medicines. Rules differ by country — check local
  guidance.
- **A shared calendar** on the fridge, with reorder dates marked, keeps
  everyone in the household aware.
- **Use a weekly pill box** for anyone taking several medicines a day, filled
  once a week, so missed doses are obvious.

## Never share prescription medicines

A medicine prescribed for one person is chosen for their body, condition,
other medicines, and allergies. Giving it to someone else — even for "the
same" problem — can cause harm, hide a serious illness, or (for antibiotics)
breed resistant germs that make the next infection harder to treat. Point the
person to a pharmacist or clinic instead.

## Step 5: Dispose of medicines safely

- **Take old and unwanted medicines back to a pharmacy** — many accept them
  free of charge.
- **Don't flush them** or pour them down the sink unless the leaflet says
  to; they end up in rivers and harm wildlife.
- **If there's no take-back scheme,** check local guidance. A common method
  is to mix them with something unappealing (used tea leaves, soil, cat
  litter), seal them in a bag, and put them in household rubbish.
- **Needles and lancets** go in a rigid, lidded sharps container, never loose
  in the bin.
- **Remove personal details** from labels before throwing packaging away.

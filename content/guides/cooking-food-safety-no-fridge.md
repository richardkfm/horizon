# Keep food safe without a fridge

People kept food safely for thousands of years before fridges, and plenty of
households still do — at a campsite, an allotment, in a power cut, or simply
because they don't have one. The secret isn't special equipment; it's a few
rules about **time and temperature**, and knowing which foods are risky and
which are almost bulletproof.

> **Risk:** Food that's gone bad doesn't always look, smell, or taste bad.
> The germs that cause food poisoning — and the toxin behind botulism — can
> be present in food that seems fine. Follow the rules, not your nose. **When
> in doubt, throw it out.**

## The danger zone

Germs multiply fastest between roughly **5 °C and 60 °C** — fridge-cold to
too-hot-to-touch. Some agencies use slightly different figures (the USDA uses
4–60 °C, or 40–140 °F); the idea is the same everywhere.

```ascii
   100 C  |  boiling - kills most germs
          |
    75 C  |  reheat to here: steaming hot throughout
          |
    60 C  +================================
          |                                 \
          |   DANGER ZONE                    |  germs double
          |   (room temperature is           |  as often as
          |    right in the middle)          |  every 20 min
          |                                 /
     5 C  +================================
          |  fridge, cold store: germs slow down
     0 C  |  frozen: germs paused, not killed
```

*Fig. 1: the danger zone — keep cooked and perishable food out of it as much
as possible: hot food hot, cold food cold, and nothing in between for long*

## The 2-hour rule

> **Spec:** Don't leave cooked or perishable food in the danger zone for
> more than **2 hours** in total — or **1 hour** if it's hotter than about
> 32 °C. After that, eat it straight away or throw it out.

- **Count all the time** it spends warm: cooling on the counter, sitting on
  the table, in a lunch box.
- **High-risk foods:** cooked rice, pasta, and grains; cooked beans and
  lentils; soups, stews, and sauces; cut melon; sprouts; tofu and soya milk
  once opened; anything with dairy or egg.
- **Low-risk foods:** whole raw fruit and vegetables, bread, dry grains and
  pulses, nuts, dried fruit, unopened tins, vinegar pickles, jam.

> **Tip:** Without a fridge, the safest leftover is **no leftover**. Cook
> only what will be eaten at one meal, or cook one big pot and share it with
> neighbours.

## Cool and reheat safely

- **Cool food fast** if you must keep it: divide it into small, shallow
  containers, set them in a basin of cold water, and stir. Big pots stay warm
  for hours.
- **Cooked rice** is a common cause of food poisoning — eat it hot, or cool
  it quickly and eat it within a day, kept cold.
- **Reheat until steaming hot all the way through** (about 75 °C), stirring
  so there are no cold spots.
- **Reheat only once.** Bring soups and stews to a full rolling boil.

## Keep things cool without power

### Cool boxes

- **Pre-chill** the box and everything going in it.
- **Block ice lasts longer** than cubes; frozen bottles of water double as
  drinking water as they melt.
- **Keep it full, closed, and in the shade,** wrapped in a blanket. Open it
  as rarely as possible.
- **Check the temperature** with a thermometer — once the inside rises above
  5 °C, the 2-hour clock starts for perishable food.

### Clay-pot coolers (zeer pots)

Evaporating water pulls heat away. A zeer pot uses this to keep vegetables
and fruit fresh for days longer in hot, dry weather.

```ascii
     wet cloth cover
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
   \  .   +--------+   . /   <- outer clay pot (unglazed)
    \ .   | food   |  . /
     \ .  | inner  | . /     <- wet sand between the pots
      \.  |  pot   |./
       \  +--------+ /
        \___________/
      shade + a breeze
```

*Fig. 2: a zeer pot — two unglazed clay pots with wet sand between them;
water evaporating from the outside cools the inner pot*

1. Put a small unglazed clay pot inside a larger one.
2. Fill the gap with sand and soak it with water.
3. Put food in the inner pot and cover with a wet cloth.
4. Stand it in shade where air moves, and re-wet the sand once or twice a
   day.

> **Avoid if:** you need to keep cooked leftovers or other high-risk food.
> A zeer pot keeps vegetables and fruit cooler than the air around it, but it
> doesn't reliably reach fridge temperature, and it works poorly in humid
> weather.

## Botulism: the rules that matter most

Botulism is rare but can be deadly. The germ grows **without air**, in
**low-acid** food, at room temperature — exactly the conditions inside a
sealed jar or tin. It can't be seen, smelled, or tasted.

- **Never keep garlic, herbs, chillies, or vegetables in oil at room
  temperature.** Keep homemade flavoured oils in a fridge and use them within
  about 4 days, or freeze them — or don't make them at all without one.
- **Never home-can low-acid foods** — vegetables, beans, soups, mushrooms —
  in a water bath or an oven. They need a **pressure canner** and a tested
  recipe.
- **Throw away** any tin or jar that's **bulging, leaking, spurting,
  badly dented, or rusty**, or smells off when opened. Don't taste it. Wrap
  it in a bag and put it in the rubbish, and wash your hands and any
  surfaces it touched.
- **Never give honey to babies under one year old** — it can carry botulism
  spores their gut can't yet handle.

> **Do now:** Get emergency help immediately if someone has **blurred or
> double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing,
> or weakness spreading down the body** after eating stored food. Botulism
> needs hospital treatment fast.

## Safe and unsafe ways to preserve

| Safe at home | Not safe |
| --- | --- |
| Drying fruit, vegetables, herbs, beans | Garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature |
| Lacto-fermenting with the right amount of salt | Water-bath or oven canning of vegetables, beans, or soup |
| Vinegar pickles from a tested recipe | "Open kettle" canning (hot food in jars, no processing) |
| Jams with plenty of sugar, water-bath processed | Sealing cooked food in jars and leaving it out |
| Water-bath canning of high-acid fruit and pickles, tested recipe | Untested recipes or halving the acid or salt |
| Pressure canning of low-acid foods, tested recipe | Reusing one-trip lids or chipped jars |

See [[cooking-preserving]] and [[cooking-ferments]] for how to do the safe
ones well.

## Clean hands, clean kitchen

- **Wash hands with soap** before cooking and eating, and after the toilet.
- **Use safe water** for washing raw food and dishes — see
  [[water-choosing-treatment]].
- **Keep raw and ready-to-eat food apart,** with separate boards if you can.
- **Store dry food sealed** against damp, insects, and mice.

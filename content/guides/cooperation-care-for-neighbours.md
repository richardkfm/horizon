# Look out for older and disabled neighbours

Every street has people who'd struggle alone in a heatwave, a power cut, or
a bad flu season — someone who lives alone, uses a wheelchair, is hard of
hearing, or is caring for a partner with dementia. A neighbourhood that
quietly looks out for them is a better place to live every day, not just in
an emergency. It doesn't take a charity or a rota app: a list, a few willing
people, and the habit of knocking on the door.

> **Decision:** Nothing about us without us. Plan *with* older and
> disabled neighbours, not *for* them. They know their own needs, routines,
> and limits better than anyone. Ask, listen, and let them decide what help
> they want — and what they don't.

## Step 1: Get to know who's around

- **Introduce yourself** and offer your phone number. Ask whether they'd like
  a neighbour to check in now and then, and how.
- **Ask what matters to them:** "Is there anything that would be hard for
  you if the power went off, or the lift broke, or it snowed?"
- **Write down only what they're happy to share** — with their permission,
  and kept private. Use the [[checklist:accessibility-emergency-plan]]
  checklist together.

## Step 2: Set up a check-in roster

A roster means nobody is forgotten when one helper is busy or away.

```ascii
   neighbour     main buddy    backup      check-in
   ------------  -----------   ---------   -----------------
   Mrs A, no.4   Sam, no.6     Ria, no.9   daily, 10 am call
   Mr B, no.12   Ria, no.9     Jo, no.15   Mon/Thu visit
   C & D, no.20  Jo, no.15     Sam, no.6   weekly, + heat
                                           and cold alerts
   ------------------------------------------------------
   window card:  GREEN = all fine today   RED = please knock
```

*Fig. 1: a simple check-in roster — every person has a main buddy and a
backup, an agreed rhythm, and an extra check during heat, cold, or outages*

1. **Pair each person with a main buddy and a backup** who live close.
2. **Agree the rhythm** with the person: a daily call, a weekly visit, a
   wave at the window.
3. **Add extra checks** during heatwaves, cold snaps, storms, power cuts,
   and illness going around.
4. **A window card** (green side "all fine", red side "please knock") lets
   people signal without a phone.
5. **Agree what happens if there's no answer:** who has a spare key, who to
   call, and when to call emergency services.
6. **Review the roster** every few months, or when anyone moves or their
   needs change.

## Step 3: Plan accessible evacuation

Ordinary evacuation plans assume everyone can walk quickly down stairs.
Make a personal plan with each person who can't.

- **Two ways out** of the home, and which one works for them (a ground-floor
  door, a ramp, a lift that may stop in a power cut).
- **Who helps, and how.** Name two people. Agree how to move them safely —
  never lift or carry someone without knowing how; you can injure both of
  you. Practise with their wheelchair or walking aid.
- **What comes with them:** medicines and their list, glasses, hearing aids
  and spare batteries, walking aid, continence supplies, chargers, and any
  power-dependent equipment — see [[health-power-dependent-care]].
- **Where they'll go**, and that the place is accessible (step-free, an
  accessible toilet, somewhere to rest).
- **How they'll get the warning** — a knock, a text, a light, a vibration
  alarm for someone who is deaf.
- **Assistance animals** come too.

## Step 4: Help with mobility aids

- **Walking sticks:** the handle should sit at wrist height when the arm hangs
  loosely. Replace a worn rubber tip — a bald tip slips.
- **Frames and wheelchairs:** check brakes, tyres, and loose screws every
  month. Inflate pneumatic tyres. Clean wheels and axles.
- **At home:** clear trip hazards (loose rugs, cables), add lighting on
  stairs and the route to the toilet at night, and a sturdy chair with arms.
- **Ramps:** a gentle slope is safer — about 1 in 12 (1 m rise over 12 m) is
  a common guideline. A steep ramp is worse than none.

> **Tip:** Borrow before you buy. Many areas have equipment loan schemes for
> wheelchairs, commodes, and bath seats. A neighbourhood can keep a small
> shared store of aids that get passed on.

## Step 5: Everyday care — continence and skin

Only do personal care that the person wants and agrees to, and keep their
dignity at the centre: close the door, explain what you're doing, and let
them do as much as they can for themselves.

- **Keep drinking.** Many people cut down on fluids to avoid toilet trips;
  that causes dehydration, constipation, and urinary infections. Encourage
  regular drinks through the day, easing off in the evening.
- **A regular toilet routine** (after meals, before bed) and a clear, lit
  route — or a commode by the bed — prevents accidents.
- **Change pads promptly,** clean gently with water and a mild soap, pat dry,
  and use a barrier cream if the skin is red.
- **Prevent pressure sores** in anyone who sits or lies most of the day: help
  them change position at least every couple of hours, use cushions to
  protect heels, hips, and the base of the spine, and check those spots daily.

> **Risk:** Skin that stays red when pressed, broken skin, or a sore that
> isn't healing needs a nurse or doctor. So do sudden confusion, a high
> temperature, or burning when passing urine — often a sign of infection in
> older people.

## Step 6: Talk well with someone living with dementia

- **Face them, at their level,** in a quiet place, and say who you are.
- **One idea at a time,** in short, simple sentences, with time to answer.
- **Don't argue or correct.** If they think it's a different year, go with the
  feeling behind it rather than the facts ("You miss your mum. Tell me about
  her.").
- **Use routines and familiar things** — the same times, the same faces, a
  clock and calendar, labels on doors.
- **Watch body language.** Distress often comes from pain, hunger, being too
  hot or cold, or needing the toilet.

## Step 7: Include people in decisions

- **Hold meetings where everyone can get to them** — step-free, seats, a
  hearing loop or a quiet room, large print, and a way to take part by phone.
- **Ask older and disabled neighbours to help plan,** not just to receive
  help. Many have decades of practical skills to share.
- See [[cooperation-run-a-meeting]] for running meetings where quiet voices
  speak first.

## Step 8: Rest the carers

Family carers often go years without a proper break.

- **Offer specific help:** "Can I sit with your dad on Thursday afternoon so
  you can go out?" is easier to accept than "let me know if you need
  anything".
- **Share the load** across a small team of regular helpers.
- **Watch for burnout:** exhaustion, irritability, getting ill often, giving
  up their own friends and interests. See [[health-mental-health-grief]].

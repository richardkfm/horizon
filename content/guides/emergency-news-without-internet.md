# Get trustworthy news when the internet is down

With no connection, two things happen at once: the flow of reliable
information stops, and the flow of *unreliable* information speeds up,
because rumour travels by mouth perfectly well. Broadcast radio fixes the
first problem. A little discipline about what you repeat fixes the second —
and the second is the one that does real damage in an otherwise ordinary
outage.

> **Note:** Assume an outage is mundane until something credible says
> otherwise. Cut cables, failed exchanges, and overloaded masts are common;
> they explain almost every outage you will live through, and they end.

## Have a receiver that doesn't need the network

| Receiver | Needs | Reaches | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Battery or wind-up radio | Batteries or your arm | Local and national stations | The default: cheap, tiny, works anywhere |
| Car radio | A car, engine off | Same, with a good aerial | Poor indoor reception, or no other radio |
| DAB/digital radio | Mains or batteries, transmitter up | National and local | Clear sound, but fails harder at the edges of coverage |
| Shortwave radio | Batteries, a wire aerial | Across borders | A regional blackout where local stations are off too |
| Your phone | Charge; some have an FM chip | FM only, via wired headphones | A backup you may already own — check yours now |

> **Tip:** Rechargeable batteries that have sat in a drawer for a year are
> usually flat. Keep a sealed pack of ordinary alkaline cells with the radio
> and test the set twice a year — the same time you check the smoke alarms.

## Write the frequencies down before you need them

Nobody remembers frequencies, and the usual way to look one up is exactly
the thing that is broken. Do this while you are connected, on paper, and tape
it to the radio:

- Your **national broadcaster's** main news frequency for your area, plus the
  regional station that covers your town — regional stations carry the local
  instructions that matter.
- The frequency your **emergency services or civil protection** use for
  public information, if your country designates one.
- One **cross-border or shortwave** station you can hear at night, in case
  the outage takes local transmitters with it.
- **Two neighbours' names and door numbers**, since half of what you will
  want to check is local anyway.

Keep a pencil beside it. Broadcast news is a stream, not a page: if you don't
note the time and what was actually said, you'll be reconstructing it from
memory an hour later, and so will everyone else.

## Sort what you hear

Most bad information in an outage isn't a lie anyone invented. It's a real
thing someone half-heard, passed on without its source, and reshaped a little
at each step — until it comes back to you as "they're saying".

```ascii
   what you hear          ask three things            what you do with it

  "the water's off       WHO said it?     ----no----> hold it. Don't pass
   all week"             (named source?)               it on as fact.
        |                     |
        |                WHEN?  (today, or
        |                 a rumour from
        |                 yesterday?)
        |                     |
        |                HOW do they know?
        |                (saw it / heard it
        |                 from someone else?)
        |                     |
        +------all three------+--------------------> pass it on WITH its
                 answered                            source and its time
```

*Fig. 1: three questions — who, when, and how they know — before anything leaves your mouth*

- **Repeat the source, never just the claim.** "The regional station said at
  two o'clock that repairs run into tomorrow" can be checked and corrected.
  "Apparently it's days" cannot, and it frightens people for no reason.
- **Separate instructions from speculation.** An official instruction — boil
  water, stay off this road — is worth passing on quickly and exactly. A
  prediction about how long something will last is worth a shrug.
- **Correct out loud, and without shame.** If you passed on something that
  turns out to be wrong, say so to the same people. A group where correcting
  yourself is normal stops rumours dead; one where it is embarrassing does
  the opposite.
- **Be careful with anything that names a group of people** as the cause.
  This is where outages turn nasty, and it is almost never true.

## Share it so others don't have to guess

One person with a radio can serve a whole street. Note the time, the station,
and the substance on a card and put it where people already walk past — see
*keep a neighbourhood informed without the internet* for the notice board and
the relay that carries it. Knocking on the doors of anyone who can't get to
the board takes ten minutes and is the most useful thing you can do with an
afternoon of no connection.

# Estimate weights and loads in building

When you build something people stand on, sleep under, or store weight on, the
numbers stop being optional — a floor or roof that fails can kill. This guide
shows how to estimate loads and think about safe spans, and just as importantly,
**when to stop and get expert help.**

> **Risk:** **Safety is not the place to cut corners.** These are estimating methods for
> small, simple structures, with generous margins. For anything large, public,
> multi-storey, or carrying many people, get a qualified engineer. When unsure,
> overbuild.

## Two kinds of load

- **Dead load:** the permanent weight of the structure itself — beams, floor
  boards, roof covering, earth.
- **Live load:** the changing weight it must carry — people, furniture, stored
  goods, snow on a roof, wind.

The structure must safely hold **dead + live load together**, on its worst day.

## Estimate the weight

1. **Work out volumes** of each material (see [[calc-area-volume|calculate area and volume]]).
2. **Multiply by the material's density** (weight per m³, in the table below)
   to get dead load.
3. **Add live load** by area: the per-m² figure for the room's use, from the
   list below, × the floor area.
4. **Total it** — this is what the floor or roof must carry.

| Material | Weight of 1 m³ |
| --- | --- |
| Softwood (pine, spruce), dry | about 500 kg |
| Hardwood (oak, beech), dry | about 700 kg |
| Water | 1,000 kg |
| Earth, cob, or adobe, dry | about 1,800 kg |
| Concrete | about 2,400 kg |
| Stone | about 2,600 kg |

Live loads to plan with:

- **Rooms people live and sleep in:** 150–200 kg on every m² of floor.
- **Stores:** work it out from what is really kept there. A metre-high stack
  of water containers or grain sacks puts 700–1,000 kg on each m².
- **Snow:** ask what is normal locally. Fresh snow weighs about 100 kg per
  m³; wet, settled snow 300–400 kg. Half a metre of settled snow is
  150–200 kg on every m² of roof.

> **Spec:** Worked example — a 3 × 4 m sleeping loft (12 m²).
> Floor boards 25 mm thick: 12 × 0.025 = 0.3 m³ × 500 = 150 kg.
> Eleven joists of 50 × 150 mm, each 3 m long: 0.25 m³ × 500 = 125 kg.
> Dead load: 275 kg. Live load: 12 × 150 = 1,800 kg.
> **Total: about 2,100 kg** — on the joists, on whatever holds up their ends,
> and on the ground under that.

## Spans and supports

- **Shorter spans are far stronger.** A beam's strength drops sharply as it gets
  longer, so add a post or wall to shorten a span rather than hoping a long beam
  holds.
- **Deeper beams are much stronger than wider ones** — depth (height) matters
  most in a joist or beam.
- **Spread the load:** more, closer-spaced joists share weight; wide footings
  stop posts sinking into the ground.
- For specific safe spans, **use a proven span table** for your timber and
  spacing, or copy a known-good local build — don't guess at the edge.
  horizon does not include span tables, because they depend on each
  country's timber grades: print the one for your country and keep it with
  your building notes before you need it.

## Build in a safety margin

- **Design for more than you expect.** A safety factor (carrying well above the
  calculated load) covers hidden flaws, weak timber, and overload. A span
  table has that margin built in, so never go beyond it; if you copy a local
  building, never make a span longer or a timber smaller than the original.
- **Account for the worst case:** a full store, a snow-laden roof, a crowd in one
  room.
- **Watch the supports and connections** — many failures are at joints, not in the
  middle of a beam. Tie things together well.

## Know your limits

Estimating gets you a safe small shed, floor, or shelter. For a home people live
in, a heavy store, or anything others rely on, treat these numbers as a first
check only and confirm with span tables, local building knowledge, or an engineer
before you trust it with lives.

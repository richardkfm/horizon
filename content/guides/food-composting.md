# Compost kitchen and garden waste

A third or more of what many households throw away is food and garden waste.
In a compost heap, that "rubbish" becomes the single best thing you can give
your soil: dark, crumbly compost that feeds plants, holds water, and keeps
the soil alive. It closes the loop — what the garden grows goes back to the
garden — and it's free.

> **Tip:** Composting is forgiving. Get the basics roughly right — a mix of
> wet and dry, some air, some moisture — and nature does the rest. You can't
> really fail; at worst it just takes longer.

## Greens and browns

Everything you compost is either a "green" or a "brown". The balance between
them is the whole trick.

| Greens (wet, rich in nitrogen) | Browns (dry, rich in carbon) |
| --- | --- |
| Fruit and vegetable peelings | Dry leaves, straw, hay |
| Coffee grounds, tea leaves | Shredded cardboard and plain paper |
| Grass clippings | Woody prunings, chopped small |
| Fresh weeds (before they seed) | Sawdust from untreated wood |
| Spent plants from the garden | Egg boxes, toilet-roll tubes |

> **Spec:** Aim for roughly **2–3 buckets of browns for every bucket of
> greens**. Too many greens and it goes slimy and smelly; too many browns and
> it sits there doing nothing.

## What to leave out

- **Cooked food, bread, oily food, and any meat, fish, or dairy** — they
  attract rats and flies.
- **Pet droppings and human waste** — they carry germs; human waste needs a
  separate, longer process (see [[survival-dry-toilet]]).
- **Diseased plants** and **seed heads or roots of persistent weeds**
  (bindweed, couch grass) — many survive composting. See
  [[food-diagnose-plant-problems]].
- **Coal ash, barbecue charcoal, glossy or coloured paper, treated or painted
  wood, and plastics** — including most "biodegradable" plastics, which need
  industrial heat to break down.

## Choose a method

```ascii
   COLD HEAP        HOT HEAP          WORM BIN
   add as you go    build at once     small spaces
   +-----------+    +-----------+     +----------+
   | newest    |    | 55-65 C   |     | food     |
   |           |    | turned    |     | worms    |
   |           |    | weekly    |     | bedding  |
   | oldest    |    |           |     +----------+
   +-----------+    +-----------+     | castings |
                                      +----------+
   6-12 months      1-3 months        2-4 months
   little work      more work,        balconies,
                    kills most        flats,
                    weed seeds        kitchens
```

*Fig. 1: three ways to compost — a cold heap is easiest, a hot heap is
fastest, and a worm bin fits in a small space*

### Cold heap (easiest)

1. **Pick a spot** on bare soil, in part shade, so worms and microbes can
   move in from below.
2. **Use a bin or a bay** — a lidded bin, a frame of old pallets, or a wire
   cage about 1 m across.
3. **Start with a layer of twiggy browns** for airflow.
4. **Add as you go,** covering every bucket of kitchen scraps with browns.
5. **Leave it.** In 6–12 months the bottom will be ready.

### Hot heap (fastest)

1. **Gather enough to build a heap at least 1 m × 1 m × 1 m at once** —
   smaller heaps don't hold the heat.
2. **Chop everything small** and mix greens and browns well as you build.
3. **Wet it** as you go so it's as damp as a wrung-out sponge.
4. **Cover it** with a lid, old carpet, or cardboard to hold heat and
   moisture.
5. **It should heat up within days** — steaming when you open it, too hot to
   keep your hand in.
6. **Turn it every week or so** — fork the outside to the middle — to add
   air and keep it hot. Ready in 1–3 months.

> **Note:** A well-run hot heap at 55–65 °C kills most weed seeds and many
> plant diseases. A cold heap doesn't — so keep weed seeds out of it.

### Worm bin (small spaces)

1. **Use a bin with drainage holes** and a lid, or a stack of trays.
2. **Fill it with damp bedding:** shredded cardboard or paper, a little soil.
3. **Add composting worms** (red worms or tiger worms, not garden
   earthworms) — ask a gardener who has one; worm bins multiply.
4. **Feed small amounts** of chopped scraps a couple of times a week, buried
   under the bedding. Go easy on citrus and onions.
5. **Keep it shaded and frost-free,** roughly 15–25 °C.
6. **Harvest the castings** from the bottom after a few months; the liquid
   that drains out, diluted about 1 to 10 with water, is a plant feed.

## Troubleshooting

- **Smelly or slimy:** too wet or too many greens. Mix in browns and turn it.
- **Dry and nothing happening:** too many browns or too dry. Add greens and
  water.
- **Flies:** bury scraps under a layer of browns.
- **Rats:** a well-run heap has no cooked food. Put fine wire mesh under the
  heap and keep the lid on — it keeps them out without harming them.

## When is it done?

- **Dark brown, crumbly,** and smells like a forest floor.
- **You can't recognise** what went in (a few twigs or eggshell bits are
  fine — sieve them out and put them back in a new heap).
- **Cool** — no heat left in the middle.

Let it sit for a few more weeks to mature, then spread it on beds as a
mulch, dig it into new beds, or mix it with soil for pots.

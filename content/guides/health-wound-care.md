# Clean and care for a wound over the following days

Stopping the bleeding is the first job; the next week or two is what decides
whether a wound heals cleanly. Most cuts and grazes heal well at home with
clean water, a clean dressing, and someone keeping an eye on them. The skill
is knowing what "healing normally" looks like — and spotting the signs that
it isn't, early.

> **Do now:** If blood is spurting, soaking through dressings, or won't stop
> with firm pressure, deal with that first — see [[health-bleeding-control]].
> Come back to cleaning once bleeding is under control.

> **Risk:** Get professional care the same day for: deep or gaping wounds
> (edges that don't come together), wounds on the face, hands, joints, or
> genitals, animal or human bites, puncture wounds (nails, thorns), wounds
> with dirt or debris you can't wash out, and any wound in someone with
> diabetes or a weak immune system. Gaping cuts close best when stitched
> within hours.

## Clean it well (the first hour)

1. **Wash your hands** with soap and water, and wear clean gloves if you have
   them.
2. **Irrigate with plenty of clean water.** Drinkable tap water or boiled
   and cooled water is ideal. Pour or squirt a steady stream into the wound
   for several minutes — the flushing is what removes germs and grit.
   A clean squeezy bottle or a plastic bag with a pinhole gives good pressure.
3. **Wash the skin around it** with soap and water, but keep soap out of the
   wound itself.
4. **Lift out visible dirt** with tweezers cleaned in boiling water or
   alcohol. Don't dig for anything deep — leave that to a clinician.
5. **Skip harsh chemicals inside the wound.** Hydrogen peroxide, strong
   iodine, and undiluted alcohol damage healing tissue; plenty of clean water
   does the job better.
6. **Pat dry around it** with a clean cloth.

> **Avoid if:** the wound is dirty or was made by something dirty. Don't
> close it tightly with strips or tape — trapped germs can cause a deep
> infection. Cover it loosely and get it seen.

## Cover it

- **Keep it moist and covered.** Wounds heal faster and scar less under a
  dressing than left to dry and crust. A thin layer of petroleum jelly under
  the dressing helps it not stick.
- **Use a non-stick pad** against the wound, held on with tape or a bandage
  — snug, not tight.
- **Small clean cuts** with edges that sit together can be held closed with
  wound-closure strips across the cut.
- **Raise a hand or foot** for the first day to ease swelling and pain.

## Change the dressing

```ascii
   day 1-2   red edge, sore, a little clear fluid   normal
   day 2-5   pain easing, edges knitting            normal
   day 5-14  scab or new pink skin, itchy           normal
   -------------------------------------------------------
   any day   pain, redness, or swelling getting     WARNING:
             WORSE, pus, red streaks, fever         get help
```

*Fig. 1: a healing timeline — a wound should feel and look a little better
each day; getting worse after the second day is the warning sign*

1. **Change it once a day,** and any time it gets wet, dirty, or soaked
   through.
2. **Wash your hands** before and after.
3. **If it sticks,** soak it off with clean water rather than ripping it.
4. **Look and sniff:** check colour, swelling, fluid, and smell, and compare
   with yesterday. A quick note in a notebook helps.
5. **Rinse gently** with clean water if there's dried fluid, then put on a
   fresh dressing.
6. **Once a scab or new skin forms** and nothing is weeping, you can leave it
   uncovered.

## Signs of infection

Some redness and soreness right at the edge is normal for the first day or
two. Watch for these instead:

- redness that **spreads** outwards, or **red streaks** running from the wound
- swelling, warmth, or pain that is **getting worse**, not better
- **pus** (thick yellow or green fluid) or a bad smell
- **fever**, chills, or feeling generally unwell

Any of these means the wound needs a clinician's look the same day — an
infection may need prescription treatment.

## Sepsis: when to get help urgently

Sepsis is the body's extreme reaction to an infection. It can follow even a
small wound and can kill within hours, so it's an emergency.

> **Do now:** Call emergency services or get to hospital immediately if
> someone with a wound (or any infection) has any of: **slurred speech or
> confusion**; **extreme shivering or muscle pain**; **passing no urine** in
> a day; **severe breathlessness**; **mottled, blue, grey, or very pale
> skin**; or says they feel like they might die. In a child, also: very
> sleepy or hard to wake, a fit, or a rash that doesn't fade when pressed.

## Tetanus

Tetanus germs live in soil and dust and can enter through any break in the
skin, especially puncture wounds and dirty wounds.

> **Note:** If the person isn't sure their tetanus vaccination is up to date,
> or it's a dirty or deep wound and their last booster was more than about
> five years ago, ask a health worker about a booster **as soon as possible**
> after the injury. Schedules differ between countries — check local
> guidance, and keep everyone's vaccination dates on their medicine list.

# Help with a birth when a midwife can't get there in time

Birth is a natural process, and the body usually knows what to do. Now and
then a baby arrives faster than anyone expected — at home, in a car, or when
roads are closed — and the people present have to help. Your job is not to
deliver the baby; it's to **keep mother and baby safe, clean, and warm,
let birth happen, and get professional help** as soon as possible.

> **Do now:** Call emergency services first, or send someone to fetch a
> midwife, doctor, or health worker. Put the phone on speaker — a
> dispatcher or midwife can talk you through every step.

> **Risk:** This guide is only for when professional help truly can't arrive
> in time. Planned births belong with a midwife or clinic. Even after a
> birth that seems to go perfectly, mother and baby both need checking by a
> professional as soon as possible.

## Is the birth close?

If the birth isn't close, the safest choice is usually to get to help.
Signs it's very close and you should stay put:

- contractions are strong and **2 minutes apart or less**
- she feels a **strong urge to push** or bear down, or feels she needs a poo
- her waters have broken and she says the baby is coming
- you can see the **baby's head** at the opening of the vagina

## Get ready (a few minutes)

1. **Wash your hands** and forearms well with soap, and put on clean gloves
   if you have them.
2. **Make a clean, warm space:** a warm room with no draughts, a clean sheet
   or towels over a waterproof layer (a shower curtain or bin bags) on the
   bed or floor.
3. **Gather:** several clean towels or blankets, a hat or cloth for the
   baby's head, a bowl or bag for the placenta, sanitary pads or clean cloths,
   and — only if needed later — clean string and a new or boiled blade.
4. **Let her choose a position:** half-sitting propped with pillows, on her
   side, or on hands and knees. Keep her calm and encourage slow breathing.

## Let the birth happen

1. **Don't pull** on the baby, and don't try to hold the birth back.
2. **As the head comes out,** support it gently with a clean towel in your
   hand. Encourage her to pant and push gently rather than hard.
3. **If the cord is around the neck** and loose, ease it gently over the
   baby's head. If it's tight, leave it — the baby can be born through it.
4. **The body usually follows** with the next contraction. Support the baby
   as it slides out — newborns are very slippery. Hold firmly but gently.
5. **Note the time of birth.**

> **Risk:** If you see the **bottom, a foot, or an arm** first, or the
> **cord comes out before the baby**, get emergency help urgently and keep
> your hands off as much as possible — don't pull. For a cord coming first,
> help her onto knees and chest (bottom raised) while you wait.

## The first minute: dry, warm, breathing

```ascii
   baby born
      |
      v
   DRY all over with a warm towel, rub back firmly
   swap the wet towel for a dry one, put on a hat
      |
      v
   SKIN-TO-SKIN on mother's bare chest, covered
      |
      v
   breathing or crying? --- yes --> keep warm, watch,
      |                             help to feed
      no (after ~30 s of drying and rubbing)
      |
      v
   head level, wipe mouth and nose,
   5 gentle breaths over mouth AND nose,
   watch chest rise; keep going, get help
```

*Fig. 1: the first minute — drying and warmth come first; most babies start
breathing as they're dried and rubbed*

- **Dry the baby straight away,** head and body, rubbing firmly. This warms
  them and stimulates breathing. Swap wet towels for dry ones.
- **Put the baby skin-to-skin** on the mother's bare chest, tummy down, and
  cover both with a dry blanket. Cover the baby's head. Newborns lose heat
  very fast.
- **Wipe the mouth and nose** with a clean cloth if there's mucus. Don't
  poke deep into the mouth.

### If the baby isn't breathing

1. **Keep rubbing** the back firmly and flick the soles of the feet.
2. If there's still no breathing after about 30 seconds, lay the baby on
   their back on a firm, warm surface, with the head level — not tilted far
   back.
3. **Seal your mouth over the baby's mouth and nose** and give **5 gentle
   puffs** — just enough to see the chest rise.
4. **Keep giving breaths** (about one every 2 seconds) and keep the baby
   warm. Follow the dispatcher's instructions, including for chest
   compressions — see [[health-choking-and-cpr]].

## The cord: wait, and you may not need to cut it

> **Tip:** There's no rush to cut the cord. Leaving it alone lets the baby
> receive extra blood from the placenta. If help is coming within an hour or
> two, you don't need to cut it at all — keep the placenta with the baby.

If you must cut it:

1. **Wait until the cord stops pulsing** — at least a few minutes, often
   longer — and it goes white and limp.
2. **Tie it tightly in two places** with clean string or strips of cloth: the
   first about 10 cm from the baby's tummy, the second about 5 cm further
   towards the mother.
3. **Cut between the two ties only with a sterile blade** — a new, sealed
   blade, or one boiled for 10 minutes. Never use an unclean knife or
   scissors.
4. **Keep the stump clean and dry,** folded outside the nappy. Put nothing on
   it unless a health worker advises otherwise.

## The placenta

- **It usually comes 5–30 minutes after the baby,** sometimes longer, with
  a few more contractions. She may need to push gently.
- **Never pull on the cord** to bring it out.
- **Catch it in a bowl or bag** and keep it for the midwife to check that
  it's complete.
- **Putting the baby to the breast** helps the womb contract and the
  placenta come away.
- **After the placenta,** firmly rub her lower tummy (below the navel) in a
  circular motion if the womb feels soft. It should firm up like a grapefruit;
  this reduces bleeding.

## Danger signs: get help urgently

> **Do now:** Get emergency help immediately for:
> - **heavy bleeding** — soaking a pad or towel in under an hour, a steady
>   flow, or large clots, or if she becomes pale, dizzy, faint, or confused
> - **a fit**, severe headache, blurred vision, or chest pain
> - **a baby who isn't breathing**, is floppy, blue or grey, grunting, or
>   breathing very fast
> - **a baby who is cold, won't feed,** or has a fever
> - **the placenta not coming** within an hour
>
> Later, fever, a foul-smelling discharge, or sudden heavier bleeding in the
> days after birth also need a health worker.

## Feeding the newborn

- **Help the baby to the breast within the first hour.** The first thick,
  yellowish milk (colostrum) is exactly what a newborn needs.
- **A good latch:** baby's tummy to mother's tummy, nose level with the
  nipple, a wide-open mouth taking in a big mouthful of breast, not just the
  nipple. Feeding shouldn't keep hurting — re-latch if it does.
- **Feed on demand,** often 8–12 times in 24 hours. Several wet nappies a day
  after the first few days is a good sign the baby is getting enough.
- **If formula is used,** make each feed with safe water: boil it, let it
  cool for no more than about 30 minutes (so it's still at least 70 °C), mix
  the formula exactly as the packet says, cool the bottle under running water,
  and test on your wrist. Use within 2 hours and throw away what's left.
  Clean and boil bottles and teats. Ready-to-feed liquid formula needs no
  water and is useful when safe water is short.
